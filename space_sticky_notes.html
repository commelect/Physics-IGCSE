The Earth

Why things stay put

Gravity pulls objects towards Earth's centre, keeping them on the surface and holding the atmosphere in place.

The Moon's orbit

The gravitational pull between Earth and Moon provides the centripetal force that keeps the Moon in its roughly circular orbit.

Day

One full rotation of the Earth on its axis takes about 24 hours, causing day and night.

Year

One full orbit of the Earth around the Sun takes about 365.25 days.

Seasons

Caused by the tilt of Earth's axis (about 23.5°). As Earth orbits, each hemisphere tilts towards or away from the Sun, changing day length and how directly sunlight hits it.

The Solar System

The Solar System

The Sun, eight planets and their moons, dwarf planets, asteroids, and comets — all held in orbit by the Sun's gravity.

Why orbits happen

Gravitational attraction provides the centripetal force that curves an object's path into an orbit, instead of it travelling off in a straight line.

Orbital speed vs radius

For objects orbiting the same body, a smaller orbital radius means a faster orbital speed and a shorter orbital period.

Comets

Follow a highly elliptical orbit around the Sun — moving fastest at their closest approach and slowest when furthest away.

Artificial satellites

Geostationary: 24-hour orbit above the equator, staying over one point (comms, weather). Polar/low-orbit: low altitude, short period, passes over the poles (monitoring, imaging).

The Sun as a Star

The Sun

An ordinary main sequence star — the closest star to Earth, and by far the largest object in the Solar System.

Nuclear fusion

In the Sun's core, hydrogen nuclei fuse together to form helium, releasing the huge amounts of energy that power the Sun.

Energy radiated

The Sun radiates energy across the electromagnetic spectrum — mostly visible light and infrared — giving Earth the light and heat that supports life.

The Sun's life stage

Currently a stable main sequence star, roughly halfway through its life cycle.

Stars

Star formation

A star forms when gravity pulls together a huge cloud of gas and dust (a nebula), which contracts and heats up.

Main sequence

Once hydrogen fusion begins in the core, a star becomes a stable main sequence star — the stage where stars spend most of their lives.

Life cycle of a Sun-like star

Main sequence → red giant → outer layers drift away → white dwarf.

Life cycle of a massive star

Main sequence → red supergiant → supernova → neutron star or black hole.

Balance within a star

A star balances gravity pulling inward against the outward pressure from fusion. When fuel runs low, gravity takes over and the star moves to its next stage.

The Universe

Galaxies

Huge collections of billions of stars held together by gravity. Our Solar System sits within the Milky Way galaxy.

Redshift

Light from distant galaxies is shifted towards longer, redder wavelengths — evidence that those galaxies are moving away from us.

Expanding universe

The further away a galaxy is, the greater its redshift and the faster it's receding — evidence that the whole universe is expanding.

The Big Bang theory

The idea that the universe began from an extremely hot, dense point and has been expanding ever since. Supported by the redshift evidence.

Light-year

The distance light travels in one year — a unit of distance, not time. Used because distances between stars and galaxies are so vast.
