Kinetic Particle Model

States of matter

Solid: particles fixed in position, vibrating. Liquid: close together, sliding past each other. Gas: far apart, moving freely at high speed.

Kinetic particle model

All particles have kinetic energy. Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of the particles in a substance.

Brownian motion

Smoke or pollen particles show random, jittery movement from being struck by fast-moving, invisible gas or liquid molecules — evidence that particles are constantly moving.

Gas pressure (particle view)

Gas pressure comes from particles colliding with the container walls. More frequent or harder collisions mean higher pressure.

Absolute zero

0 K = −273°C. The theoretical lowest possible temperature, where particles have minimum kinetic energy.

Temperature scales

T (K) = θ (°C) + 273

A change of 1 K is the same size as a change of 1°C.

Gas Laws

Pressure law

p / T = constant

At constant volume, the pressure of a fixed mass of gas is directly proportional to its temperature in kelvin.

Boyle's law

p V = constant

At constant temperature, the pressure of a fixed mass of gas is inversely proportional to its volume.

Volume–temperature law

V / T = constant

At constant pressure, the volume of a fixed mass of gas is directly proportional to its temperature in kelvin.

Explaining it with particles

Heating a gas gives particles more kinetic energy, so they hit the walls more often and harder — raising pressure, or pushing the gas to expand, depending on what's held constant.

Thermal Expansion

Thermal expansion

Most solids, liquids, and gases expand when heated. Gases expand the most, then liquids, then solids.

Explaining it with particles

Heating raises particle kinetic energy, so particles vibrate more (solids) or move faster and spread out (liquids/gases) — increasing their average separation.

Expansion gaps

Bridges, railway tracks, and overhead power lines are built with small gaps or slack so they can expand on hot days without buckling.

Bimetallic strips

Two different metals bonded together expand at different rates, so the strip bends when heated. Used in thermostats and fire alarms.

Specific Heat Capacity

Specific heat capacity

E = m c Δθ

The energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1°C (or 1 K).

Measuring it

Supply a known electrical energy (E = VIt) to a known mass of the substance and measure the resulting temperature rise.

Why it matters

A substance with a high specific heat capacity — like water — heats up and cools down slowly, which is useful for storing thermal energy.

Change of state

Melting and boiling happen at constant temperature. The energy supplied breaks bonds between particles rather than raising their kinetic energy.

Evaporation vs boiling

Evaporation happens at any temperature, only at the liquid's surface, and cools the liquid — the fastest-moving particles escape, lowering the average kinetic energy left behind.

Thermal Energy Transfer

Conduction

Vibrating particles pass energy to their neighbours. Good in solids, especially metals — their free electrons carry energy quickly. Poor in gases.

Convection

Thermal energy is carried by the movement of a fluid itself. Heated fluid becomes less dense and rises, replaced by cooler, denser fluid — a convection current.

Radiation

Thermal energy transferred as infrared electromagnetic waves. Needs no medium, so it works through a vacuum.

Comparing surfaces

Dark, matte surfaces absorb and emit infrared radiation well. Light, shiny surfaces reflect more and are poor absorbers and emitters.

Reducing heat loss

Insulation cuts conduction; a vacuum flask's gap cuts conduction and convection; a silvered surface cuts radiation.
