Atomic Structure

Structure of the atom

A tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus (protons + neutrons) surrounded by electrons in shells. The atom is mostly empty space.

Subatomic particles

Proton: +1, mass 1, in nucleus. Neutron: 0, mass 1, in nucleus. Electron: −1, negligible mass, orbits nucleus.

Alpha-particle scattering

Most alpha particles passed straight through gold foil; a few deflected sharply or bounced back — showing a tiny, dense, positive nucleus with mostly empty space around it.

Proton (atomic) number, Z

The number of protons in the nucleus. It defines which element the atom is.

Nucleon (mass) number, A

The total number of protons plus neutrons in the nucleus.

Neutral atoms

Number of electrons = number of protons, so the atom has no overall electric charge.

Isotopes & Notation

Isotopes

Atoms of the same element (same proton number) with different numbers of neutrons, so a different nucleon number.

Nuclide notation

ᴬZX

A = nucleon number (top), Z = proton number (bottom), X = element symbol.

Ions

Atoms that have lost or gained electrons, giving an overall charge. The number of protons stays the same.

Stable vs unstable nuclei

Some isotopes have a stable balance of protons and neutrons. Unstable (radioactive) isotopes decay, emitting radiation, to become more stable.

Types of Radiation

Alpha (α)

2 protons + 2 neutrons; charge +2. Least penetrating — stopped by paper or skin. Most strongly ionising. Deflected slightly by fields.

Beta (β)

A fast electron from the nucleus (a neutron becomes a proton). Charge −1. Stopped by a few mm of aluminium. Deflected more than alpha, the opposite way.

Gamma (γ)

High-energy electromagnetic wave from the nucleus. No charge, no mass. Most penetrating — needs thick lead or concrete to stop. Not deflected by fields.

Detecting radiation

A Geiger–Müller (GM) tube and counter detects radiation and measures the count rate.

Background radiation

Present all the time, from natural sources (rocks, cosmic rays, food) and artificial ones (medical, nuclear industry). Subtract it to find a source's true reading.

Half-Life & Decay

Radioactive decay

Random and spontaneous. You can't predict when a particular unstable nucleus will decay, and physical or chemical conditions don't affect it.

Half-life

The time for half the undecayed nuclei in a sample to decay — or for the activity/count rate to fall to half its original value.

Alpha decay

Nucleon number falls by 4, proton number falls by 2, and an alpha particle (⁴₂He) is emitted.

Beta decay

Nucleon number stays the same, proton number rises by 1 (a neutron becomes a proton), and a beta particle is emitted.

Uses, Dangers & Safety

Uses of radiation

Alpha: smoke detectors. Beta: controlling material thickness. Gamma: medical tracers/treatment, sterilising, checking welds.

Dangers

Ionising radiation can damage or kill living cells, and cause mutations that may lead to cancer.

Safety precautions

Minimise exposure time, maximise distance from the source, use shielding, store sources in lead-lined containers, and handle them with tongs, never bare hands.

Contamination vs irradiation

Contamination: unwanted radioactive material getting onto or into something, causing ongoing exposure. Irradiation: exposure to radiation without becoming radioactive.
